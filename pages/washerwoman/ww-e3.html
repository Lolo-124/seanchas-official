The Washerwoman

“Give me your name, child, and I’ll send both of you home.”

She looked cannily out from under her brows. When she held her hand out to him, Kester could see the little webbing between her fingers.

He went to shake it, and she slapped his hand down. “Your name. Quickly. If the sun sets on you here, you’ll both have to stay.”

Kester blurted his name out in a panic, and felt as though something loosened in his chest, like a knot being untied.

The Bean Nighe crowed with laughter and stepped swiftly around him to collect her washing basket. Over her shoulder she called out to him as she waded into the burn, “Remember, you must leave before the night fully sets in.”

The trees around the boy shivered and the mist curled closer, but through the dimming light he could see his brother walking closer. He lunged forward and grabbed Davie’s wrist, then turned to drag him up the banks back to the path.

The light was leaving faster, and he could hardly see in front of him. As he ran, still dragging Davie behind him, he explained between heaving breaths what had taken place by the riverbank. Davie just nodded, dazed.

They crossed the forest’s threshold just as the last of the light dimmed out. It was pitch black in the field at the edge of the forest, and neither of the boys really felt much like talking. They walked home through the fog in silence.

Davie got fussed over when they returned, but their mum seemed to recognize how exhausted they were and shelved most of her questions for the morning.

In the morning, the older boy realized what he had truly given up at the burn’s side. Though people could see he was there, their gazes slid off him like water. Though he was present, they never made contact. When they spoke he could read their lips, but the only sound to reach his ears was the rushing of the burn.
