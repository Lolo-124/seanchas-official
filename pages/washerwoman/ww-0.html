The Washerwoman

Warmth steamed in the air as Kester trundled into the kitchen, washed his hands, and lifted his bowl and spoon from the drying rack.

Mum was sour in the mouth, but pressed a kiss into his hair as he filled his bowl with stew.

As he sat to eat at their table, Mum grumbled something under her breath and then began to fix the knot of her apron. “And you both are chuffed with yourselves, aye?”

Kester shook his head and shovelled too big a bite of stew into his mouth. No idea what she was on about, and he was far too hungry to be concerned with it yet.

“It stopped being a laugh around noon and so you’ll be serving yourself well to tell me. Where has Davie gone?” Her eyes were sharp under her glowering brow.

“I amn’t sure, Mum. I haven’t seen him since this morning, honest.”

They were often planning little tricks, but his younger brother was often the mastermind. Kester hadn’t minded being left out of the plot when Davie had hidden his book bag at the bus stop and legged it into the woods. One of Kester’s friends was planning to bring a brand-new football to school and that wasn’t a game he was willing to pass up.

“You two always have something gey unco planned. Are you sure you don’t know?” She grew sharp now. “If you’re too full of your games to be honest then instead of your dessert today, you’ll go out and find him, won’t you?”

Out the window the sun was beginning to descend, and the wet October chill was forbidding after dark. Davie wasn’t so dedicated to his jokes that he’d forgo the comforts of a warm tea and a dry house. Why hadn’t he come back yet?

Mulishly Kester turned back to Mum, bent close over his stew like she might take it. “He’ll come home soon surely. Must I go look for him?”

“You can either go bring him in or I’ll have your dad give you a thick ear when he’s home.”

There was nothing else for it.

Kester finished his bowl and scrubbed it clean, then skooshed out the door to begin his search. The sun would only keep setting and he had maybe an hour of good daylight left. The longer he waited the harder it would get; it was bitterly cold after the sun set. At least it had been an uncommonly dry day.

He headed for the woods, taking the long way so that he could retrieve Davie’s bag on his way. It would be his head on the block if it got nicked.

The haar rose and thickened as he made his way further into the forest, curling around the trees like hands and slipping across the game trail they always followed. Clammy air raised goosebumps on his skin before getting snatched away by the wind. There was something queer about the trees. Kester’s dinner sat leaden in his stomach, the warmth tight and sore as he braced against the cold wind. His mum didn’t like talk of the fair folk – it was unchristian – but on nights like tonight Kester couldn’t help but feel as though there must be something fae out there in all those trees. It felt wrong to yell, and he kept his voice tempered as he called out for his brother.

Then he shook himself. He was twelve, and everyone knew that was far too old for fairy tales. Still he jumped when a stray nettle nipped his wrist where the jacket cuff didn’t quite reach his pocket.

It was truly the gloaming now, and as the light dimmed Kester began to rely more on his other senses. The trees creaked and chattered overhead, and the little birds called to each other as they settled into their roosts. The rowans and alders shook like old men in the wind and the brambles on the side of the trail dug their little fingernails into the knit of his jumper as Kester forged on blind. The burn was ahead and if Davie wasn’t in their fort Kester didn’t know where next he would look.

The sounds changed then. The trees seemed to hush just a bit, and Kester could hear another person over the noise of burbling water. It didn’t sound like Davie. Kester raised his voice to call out again just in case.

As Kester left the path to come to the burn’s edge, he found a doddery old woman hunched over a basket of washing. She was tiny, small enough that Kester stood a half-head above her even at twelve, and she looked like she was wearing a wrap of weathered and patchworked tea towels.

She looked up at him. When she grinned at him her teeth were very small and very sharp.

“Are you looking for something?” Her eyes were little black beetles hidden in the nest of her wrinkled face.

Kester didn’t answer immediately. There was something strange in her expression and it made him feel small and shy. It was weird as well that she was doing her washing so late in the evening. The burn’s water was baltic and the light was nearly gone. His stomach sank.

She must have been one of the fair folk.

He tried to recall the whispered stories he had caught sections of at school and at the holidays when the old folk would get drunk and chatty.

She stayed silent, waiting. He looked closer at her basket. The jumper she was washing looked exactly the same as Davie’s. It was too dark to see colour, but his mother’s knitting was not very even and he recognized the gapping on the shoulder seams.
