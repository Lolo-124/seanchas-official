The Washerwoman

With trembling hands Kester took the jumper from the crone.

The fabric was, to his surprise, warm and soft under his fingers. The crone’s eyes glimmered through the gloomy dusk as he stood there holding it. He waited as she walked away into the river, and above the rushing water he heard her voice fading. “Do not let the dark set in fully while you are within the trees, or I’ll have you both. And there are worse things out here.”

Kester backpedalled onto the path, frantic, as soon as she was gone. He folded the jumper neatly and set it on a stone to the side of the path. He wasn’t sure if this would work, but Davie came ambling out of the fog further up the path.

The light was so dim now that Kester didn’t even bother to say anything, only snatched Davie’s wrist – he was clammy – and dragged him off the bank and back onto the path. Branches scratched their faces and roots seemed to rise out of the gloom to trip them. Kester kept a hand on Davie’s back, shoving him wordlessly forward even as Davie tried to ask questions. The mist was so thick now that Kester could only see the path a couple steps ahead, and the woods around them seemed to crowd even closer.

Davie finally seemed to pick up on his brother’s mood and started to run faster. They stumbled occasionally but the path began to get gradually wider and finally they burst out of the trees and onto the brambled overgrowth at the edge of the woods.

As he crossed the boundary of the stone border wall, Davie tripped over something and went sprawling. Kester tripped over him.

For a while they both lay on the muddy path.

“Why on earth have you got my jumper on?”

Cold washed over Kester and he sat up, hurriedly dragging it over his head by the back of the neck. Hadn’t he set it to the side of the path? He wasn’t about to go back and check.

“Suits you right for disappearing all day. Where were you and why were you gone so long?”

Davie’s nose crunched up. “What do you mean ‘all day’? It’s hardly been half an hour, though it does look very dark.”

Kester felt quite irritable. He’d had the fright of his life, he was cold and hungry again and his muscles hurt, and now Davie didn’t even seem to grasp how much trouble he’d made for them both.

“It’s been all day, Davie…”

He paused. He’d been about to tell him about the old woman, but he didn’t want his brother to think he was being silly, and worse Davie might tell Mum, and then he’d be grounded from going round to Grant’s for telly on the weekends.

Davie nudged for Kester to finish his sentence.

“And Mum’s quite cross about you not being home for tea. She sent me out to get you.”

Davie blanched. He groaned to his feet and then headed back towards the house, whose cheerful little kitchen lights winked out through the dark at them.
