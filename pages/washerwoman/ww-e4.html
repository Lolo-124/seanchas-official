The Washerwoman

Her hand was shockingly cold, even against his bloodlessly numb fingers.

“Good bairn,” she said, “we’ll be home shortly.”

There was a strange sort of resonance in her voice now, replacing the crackling rasp from before. It soothed Kester’s nerves. His shoulders dropped. She gestured to the basket.

“Won’t you carry that for me please? My arms ache from washing.”

Dutifully Kester heaved the basket up onto one hip with his free arm, still holding tightly to the woman’s hand.

As they walked, she reminded him of the rules she said she reminded him of often. He was a forgetful child, but that was all right. He mustn’t cross the running water, for he would be swept away, and he mustn’t cross any bridges, for beyond them lay a land forbidden to them both. He must always come when she called, and in return for her kindness in raising him, he must do the washing daily.

Davie woke up alone in the field behind his home on the edge of the wood the next morning. Confused, he brushed himself off and made his way home to be fussed over and given a row, and as is common with young children, soon forgot about the strangeness of that day. As far as anyone was aware he had always been an only child.
