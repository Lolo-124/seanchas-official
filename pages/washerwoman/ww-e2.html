The Washerwoman

The only thing worse than being some sort of servant would be Mum’s face when he got back and told her that some old lady had taken Davie and Kester hadn’t done anything about it.

Fear sat like lead in his stomach.

“What would become of me?”

The air blew colder and the leaves above Kester’s head seemed to chatter nervously.

The old lady cackled. “Do release me, child. I won’t hurt you. A fostered child is never hungry and never alone. They just cannot return home. I will keep you well, and all I need of you is some help with the chores.”

Kester swallowed. He wasn’t sure what to do. “And what if I say no?”

The woman looked cross. “I’ll have to let you go, but I’ll also need to kill the other. And that causes so much washing. And I’ll still not have a debt paid to me.” She shook her head and muttered to herself about how boys always died so messily, turning away from Kester and shuffling over to her washing basket.

“I’ll go.”

Kester hadn’t meant to speak so rashly, but he couldn’t let Davie just die. He’d find a way to escape. He’d think of something.

The Bean Nighe whirled round, and as her skirt lifted slightly Kester caught a glimpse of scaly, webbed feet like a frog’s. He shuddered.

“Very good then,” her voice was gleeful as she held out her hand as if to shake, “and may I have your name?”

Her fingers were slightly webbed too.

“Kester.”

She grinned again and her sharp teeth made her look unearthly. “Excellent, my boy. Your brother is headed home safely. Now you and I have all this washing up to finish before we head home for the night. Be a dear and help me finish up, won’t you?”

The boy didn’t see why he shouldn’t. Besides, who was this brother she spoke about? All he wanted was to make sure Mother felt taken care of.

Across the wood, Davie trudged home with his hands in his pockets. He hadn’t meant to get so carried away, but somehow it had gotten dark without him noticing, and he’d lost his jumper too. Mum would be cross. She was often quite protective of him, and since he was her only child she worried more than his friends’ mums did. He dreaded the row that was waiting, but it was getting colder, and starting to rain. He walked a little bit faster.
