The Washerwoman

Kester fought down the urge to follow the old lady with all of his strength.

He sat down, scrubbed his hands and his feet down into the stones and silt around him, and grit his teeth against the nausea that rose inside him.

The stones were cold beneath him, and the low, spindly branches from trees behind him lashed against his body in the howling wind. He could hear the woman’s splashing as she waded across the burn and didn’t dare open his eyes.

She didn’t seem to get any quieter, and the urge to follow didn’t die down, but only grew stronger.

Finally the splashing stopped. He waited a while longer, feeling his fingers begin to numb in the cold. The air was freezing.

Eventually his patience ran out and gingerly he gathered his limbs underneath himself and worked up the nerve to crack his eyes open.

It was pitch black, and slowly a face began to take shape in front of him, vague and immaterial like the shapes that swim behind closed eyes. It looked uncannily like the Bean Nighe, and he was grateful that this was only a mirage brought on by the deep night around him.

Then she grinned.

Kester yelped like a kicked hound and scrabbled backwards away from her.

She leered at him as he flailed up the bank and away from the water. “You are not a brave child. You won’t ever be free of this night.”

He could hear her footsteps splash in the water again, but he was already sprinting headlong up the path as fast as his useless rubber wellies would take him.

He did not look back once the entire way home and only slowed briefly to rip a handful of rowan leaves from a tree as he went. Just in case, he shouted an apology over his shoulder to the tree. So many strange things had happened already, he wouldn’t risk upsetting something else.

His breath sawed through his lungs. The cold breeze was a relief against his sweating brow. His legs were rubbery and he stumbled repeatedly. But he could see the lights from the house, and he did not slow down until he barrelled through the door into the kitchen.

Mum was sat at the table, head in her hands, and the sight of her was so good and so comforting that Kester burst into tears.

His mum made her way around the table to crouch at the puddle he made on the floor. She extricated him from his boots, stripped his damp clothes off of him, and before he could blink she had scrubbed the dirt from his hands, re-dressed him in clean clothes, and wrapped him in a throw by the fire with a hot drink.

Kester had quieted to the occasional sniffle. Mum sat beside him, an arm around his shoulder. She was so warm.

“He’ll come home, Kester, dinna fash about it.” Her voice was soft. “You did a good job looking for him. There’s nothing else to be done tonight.”

“Don’t make me go to sleep.” Kester did not have the energy to beg, though the idea of being alone in the darkness made him want to weep even if he was in his bed.

Thankfully, Mum did not fight him. She sat up with him till the wee hours. Eventually the repetitive tumbling of the fire in the hearth and the rocking of his mother breathing beside him sent him dozing off.

He was woken in the early morning when she shifted him aside to stand. He scrambled to his feet after her, and settled on the floor by her rocking chair near the door. She ran a hand through his hair while they waited, door open to the rising dawn. Eventually, up the path they saw Davie come, walking in a daze.

As he got near, Kester and his mum rushed out to collect poor Davie, and helped him get warm and dry.

Mum sat with him coddled against her in her lap while Kester plied him with blankets and a mug of tea.

Davie never said a word. His stare was almost vacant, and though he acknowledged their speaking to him, it was as though something in him was missing. His smile was almost chilling now, devoid of any emotion. Just hollow.

He never spoke again.
