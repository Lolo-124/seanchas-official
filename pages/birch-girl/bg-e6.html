The Birch Girl

“No.”

It came out sharper than she’d meant, and she tempered her tone when she tried again. “I appreciate this, but no, I’ll find wood myself.” She couldn’t take it.

The girl’s wooden limb dropped. She didn’t look angry, but she wasn’t pleased. “To turn down a gift is disrespect,” she said. “Go home, hen, and mind yourself.”

Eilidh backed away and snatched the knife up from the roots without wrapping it. Back along the top of the dyke, as she walked to the knowe, she collected dead brash, windfallen birch limbs, and built it into a clumsy fire there. The lighter scraped four times before the flame would stand, and the windfall birch took reluctantly. It puttered weakly but burned nonetheless.

With the light cast from the weak fire, she crept along the edges of the woods, gathering more birch until she’d piled enough to last through the night.

After the sun sank came the bells, small and bright, ringing far across the glen and echoing in through the trees. She did not turn around.

She had gathered enough birch to last the night, but the bells were closer now, and the grass around her fire was wet enough that it wouldn’t spread. She left the fire to burn itself out and ran, Bess at her heel, breath sore in her throat, and did not look back once the whole way home. The smoke clung to her hair all the way down. It was the last time she would smell woodsmoke on herself.

Gran was awake when she got home. “You did well, lass,” she said. When Eilidh took her hand, it was cold.

The folk didn’t take Gran, they took something else. It began the next morning when the cooker clicked and clicked and wouldn’t light. Dad swapped it for an electric one, but it died. The hearth wouldn’t take a match. The logs sat like stones in the grate.

In autumn they moved to a flat in Perth with radiators that wouldn’t heat, and the whole family wore their coats at the table. Eilidh’s hands were always cold.
