The Birch Girl

Gran’s room reeked of Vicks. Eilidh set the tea tray on the bedside table, careful of the rattling cup, and was turning to the door when a hand caught her wrist. The fingers were frail as sparrow bones.

“What’s the date, hen?”

“The thirtieth. Gran, you should drink that while it’s—”

“Beltane’s eve.” Gran’s chest hitched and rattled, and she waited for it to settle before she went on. “Is there a fire on the knowe?”

There was not. Dad was away helping his brother move house down south, Mum had left for her night shift at the care home, and Eilidh had been left in charge of Gran, the collie, and her chemistry homework, in that order.

“Nobody’s done that in years, Gran.”

“I’ve done it every year of my life, and my mother before me.” The grip tightened. “They come for the ones with a foot out the door, Eilidh. We need a wee fire of birk wood, lit before the sun’s down, so the smoke tells them this house is kept. I canna get up the hill. You’ll have to do it.”

Eilidh opened her mouth to say it was superstition. Then she saw the blue tint in Gran’s lips, and the way her eyes kept sliding past things that weren’t there, and she shut it again. If Gran worked herself up any more she’d be back on the oxygen, and the ambulance took forty minutes to get out to them on a good day.

“Fine. A wee fire. Birk, mind. Nothing else will do. The older is better. They grow on the edge of the wood, in that old ditch.” Gran’s eyes were closing. “And take your father’s big knife from the shed.”

“For the branches?”

“For anything. Iron’s iron, hen. Make sure you add a drop of blood to the fire, so they know it’s your family as is watched over.”

The evening was bright and bitter, the way April evenings in the glen always were, with rain all morning and the sky now scrubbed clean and briefly blue. Eilidh stuffed newspaper, a lighter, and a foil-wrapped wedge of bannock from the bread bin into her rucksack, wrapped the knife’s serrated blade in a tea towel and laid it in beside them, and set off up the old road with Bess trotting at her heel.

Lambs bleated in the fields on either side. A curlew went up out of the rushes with that long falling call of theirs. Lovebugs landed on Eilidh’s arms and she flicked them off. The sun sat a hand above the western ridge.

The ditch was farther than she remembered. Where the march dyke ran along the edge of the wood, with the ditch beside it, Bess sat down. She didn’t whine or growl or react in the ways Eilidh would have expected. She simply sat with her ears flat, looking at the wood beyond the stones, and would not be coaxed to continue.

“Fine,” Eilidh huffed, part frustrated, part amused. “Feartie.”

The birches on the far side were silver and just coming into leaf. The woods should have been loud with chaffinches, but it was so quiet she could hear the knife knocking against her water bottle with every step.

It made her think of the stories of fair folk. She had half listened to the old folk’s stories at bonfires and to her maths teacher’s at school, the way you half listen to anything you’re sure is made up. Cold iron kills them. Never thank them. Never eat their food. Never give your name. Be polite but never trust their goodwill. That seemed an awfully alienating way to treat someone.

The old birch leaned out of the ditch the way a nosy woman peers over a fence. It was enormous, its bark peeling in papery curls and scarred with black marks, its crown green against the sky.

When a bough moved against the wind, Eilidh realized that the trunk had shoulders, and a face.

Her skin was the thin, pale white of birch paper, with dark seams along her cheekbones and joints. Her hair grew in thin branches out of her scalp the way grass grows. Her limbs were the limbs of the tree and her torso and legs were one unit growing up out of the ground. Though the tree was vast, the girl seemed no older than Eilidh.

Her eyes were birch lenticels, dark as peat water, and she watched Eilidh come closer without any surprise at all. The air smelled of sap and the sun-warmed fields.

“I know what you’ve come for,” the birch girl said, in a voice that almost rustled.

Eilidh’s hand had found the strap of her bag and the weight inside it reassured her.

She hadn’t said a word yet. The girl hadn’t asked her anything. The sun had slid another finger down the sky, and the whole wood had gone as hushed as a held breath. Something crept up Eilidh’s spine, slow and cold.

The girl waited. Eilidh looked at the bag, at the girl, at the sun sinking, and tried to remember which of the folktale rules were meant for this.
