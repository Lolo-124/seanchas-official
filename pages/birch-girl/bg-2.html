The Birch Girl

Eilidh wasn’t sure her choice was right, but she had to try something.

The knife was out of the tea towel and she was climbing down into the ditch and across the roots before she could let herself second-guess. She reached as high as she could and set the flat of the blade against the bark just below the girl’s waist. The points on the edge bit in a hair’s breadth.

The girl went very still. She looked down at the iron blade, and then up at Eilidh. Her eyes were old and tired in her youthful face.

“I know what you are,” Eilidh said. Her voice shook. “I’ve got iron with me. You’ll give me—” her voice petered out.

The girl kept staring, with no expression other than a slight tightening around her eyes from the pain of the knife. She did not speak. The old folk pressed that it was essential, when encountering the fae, to be mean enough to get an answer, but nobody had ever mentioned what to do after you got there. Sap welled clear and slippery from the cut and ran down the bark.

Then the tree boughs began to lower.

First one, then ten, then more than she could count, silver and slender, curving down around her on every side like the fingers of a closing hand. The leaves rattled. The green light dimmed. Where the sun had been a finger above the ridge, there were only slivers of it between the branches, and then fewer.

She was caged.

The birch girl stayed silent.
