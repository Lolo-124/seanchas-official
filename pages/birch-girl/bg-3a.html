The Birch Girl

She wouldn’t waste the chance.

Eilidh shoved as many fallen branches as she could grab into her bag, snatched her knife from between two roots and shoved it in after them, left the zip open, and clambered back up the side of the ditch.

Her palms were scratched and smarting, but Eilidh didn’t have time to check for blood.

She raced up to the knowe in the setting light, and though her hands were shaking she managed to click the lighter open and light the newspaper she’d shoved into the middle of the pile of thin branches. Her palms were bloody enough that she didn’t worry about making a cut in her finger. It was smeared over the wood already.

The sun had set.

A thin line of pale smoke twisted up into the sky and was whisked away. The birch bark caught like paper, curling back on itself, and the smoke it gave off was sweet.

Now that the fire was started, and just in time, Eilidh could relax enough to think. The fire needed to last the night through. She only had a little wood, and she wasn’t sure how long it would burn for. She would need to keep the fire small.

The air was still cold and a breeze was picking up. Eilidh was glad to have brought her thickest jumper, as even with it the night would be long and freezing. She gathered stones from around the top of the knowe to bank against the wind, and with caution pulled the unlit branches back out of her pile to set aside, so that she could feed the fire through the night.

It was properly dark now. She fed the fire when it started to dim, but tried to ration the wood. Somewhere in the long middle of the night she dug the bannock out of her bag and ate it a corner at a time, the oatmeal dry and faintly sweet, making it last.

Her phone battery was dead and she had no idea how long she had until the sunrise. Eilidh kept herself busy by playing little games with pebbles she’d fished out of the grass around her, then braiding the long grasses that poked through the moss at her feet, and then by making little towers out of the extra birch wood, and cutting the twigs into uniform pieces. The less she let herself peer out into the black forest the less panicked she felt.

When she sat back onto her heels from adding to the fire again, she realized the land around her had gone completely silent. She was in a little clearing near the edge of the forest, but there were none of the usual sounds of night. The bugs were silent, nothing rustled in the underbrush, and even the trees seemed to hold their breath. Eilidh felt suddenly very small and very exposed.

A ringing came, from far off into the woods, chiming regularly and getting louder. She put another branch onto the small fire and sat as close to it as she could.

Closer and closer the bells came, until they seemed to come from all around her. They circled the knowe for an eternity, then trailed off into the trees again.

Soon after the fair folk left, grey light began to creep back over the horizon. Eilidh could have cried for how grateful she suddenly was to live so far north. Nights at this time of year were only six hours or so.

Impatient, she waited for the sun to rise above the horizon. Her wood pile was dwindling. Her toes were numb with cold and her nose was running. She packed her bag again with the extra wood and moved the stones back to where they’d come from.

As soon as the light broke, she stamped the fire out and started hurrying for home, ash worked into her cuffs and the smell of birch smoke clinging to her hair. What if something had happened to Gran? And she was knackered.
