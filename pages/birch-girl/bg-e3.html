The Birch Girl

“Blood,” Eilidh said, because time was something

she couldn’t afford to lose. She held out her hand.

The girl took it and turned the palm upwards in her own wooden one. She took a twig as sharp as a thorn and drove it through the middle of Eilidh’s hand. The pain was excruciating and only the girl’s iron grip around her wrist kept her on her feet. Blood ran down her forearm and dripped off her elbow onto the roots.

The cage lifted as the fairy released Eilidh’s wrist. Then the whole tree shook and a rain of dry brash fell down around her, enough and extra for a fire. Eilidh wrapped her bleeding hand in the fallen tea towel and scooped up as much of the kindling as she could.

She caught words of thanks just in time, and ran up the path to the knowe. Her hand was throbbing. The lighter scraped twice under her good thumb before the flame stood up. The dry birch caught like paper, curling back on itself, and she tended it all night without much trouble. Near dawn she unwrapped the bannock and ate it one-handed, the smoke sweet in her hair.

When Gran asked how the night had gone, Eilidh told her it was fine and went to bed to sleep the whole rest of the day. Her hand was unmarked by the time she awoke, and Eilidh wrote the whole event off as a bad dream until a few weeks later when she skinned her knuckle open on a vegetable peeler.

A few bright drops of blood fell onto the linoleum, and by tea time a sapling had sprouted up.

She ripped it from the ground and threw it out, but by morning more had sprouted and despite every effort to get rid of them, eventually she and her family were forced to move house.

She did her best to be careful, but nobody lives without bleeding: a nosebleed in the cold, a grazed knee, a paper cut. Everywhere she lived was a small silver wood within the year.
