The Birch Girl

“A handful,” Eilidh said, “is plenty.”

Everything she’d heard said that a debt to the Folk was never safe to carry, and a handful was a debt she could repay. It would be enough for Gran, too.

She had no scissors, and she wasn’t going to pick up the knife again, so she took a small section of hair from her own head and wound it round her finger. With a jerk, it came away. It hurt more than she’d expected it to, but she held it out to the birch girl anyway, who stared. Eilidh faltered.

“Hair for hair,” she said.

Then the birch girl moved. She took the lock from Eilidh and wove it into the green above her wooden temple, where it caught and stuck out among the leaves.

“It’s rare to find one who would answer a gift in kind. This wood will burn through the night if you tend it.”

Eilidh took her branches with her and sprinted clumsily up to the knowe, setting her match to the birch bark just as the sun dropped below the horizon.

The sprigs took at the first touch of the flame, their leaves curling back on themselves like paper, and the smoke that rose was sweet and straight. The folk did not come. Dusk passed gently over the knowe. Somewhere in the small hours she ate the bannock a corner at a time to stay awake, and when dawn came, the fire went out on its own and Eilidh went home.

Gran woke with clear lungs. The patch on Eilidh’s scalp grew in silver-white and stayed that way. In the winters to come, she felt the first hard frost in her own bones weeks before the forecast, and every April, she would wait with aches when the buds broke. She couldn’t go more than a day’s travel from the glen without a migraine, but when the house was quiet, she could walk up the path to the ditch, and the birch tree was there.

They never spoke much, but Eilidh brought her small things: a bit of ribbon, or small gifts. The tree would ask after Gran’s health.
